Rezo Shmertz (Revaz Shmertz)

European Court of Human Rights judgment

On 23 November 2023, the European Court of Human Rights delivered its final judgment in Mazanov and Others v. Russia. My application appears as Revaz SHMERTS, no. 2976/22, lodged on 7 December 2021 and listed as entry 10 in the appendix. The entry lists 1980 as my birth year and Yekaterina Viktorovna Yefremova of Moscow as my representative.

Article 5 § 1 — unrecorded detention. The Court found a violation in connection with my detention for more than three hours without a written record on 8 June 2021. The appendix lists 8:05 a.m. to 9:00 p.m. and notes that the arrest record gives 8:55 p.m. as the allegedly incorrect arrest time.

Article 5 § 3 — excessive pre-trial detention. The Court found a violation concerning my pre-trial detention on fraud charges, which began on 8 June 2021 and was still ongoing on 16 September 2022. The appendix identifies the Tverskoy District Court and the Moscow City Court. It records weak grounds for continued detention, assumptions about flight or obstruction of justice without evidentiary support, and a failure to consider alternatives to detention. The Court awarded me EUR 3,900.

Russia ceased to be a party to the European Convention on Human Rights on 16 September 2022. The Court retains jurisdiction over relevant acts or omissions up to that date.

My position on the proceedings

Before my prosecution, I acted as the representative of an injured party formally recognised as a victim in proceedings concerning the same properties. Documents showing payment for the property were submitted in my defence. My position is that the courts disregarded this evidence and the fundamental contradiction between my earlier role and the subsequent case against me.

I regard the entire process—from detention through trial and conviction—as fundamentally unfair. In my assessment, the detention violations, the earlier victim status, the disregard of evidence supporting my defence and the pressure described by my defence counsel undermine the fairness and reliability of the conviction delivered in September 2023.

Wider context on Russian criminal justice

In its country-information response Q46-2023, completed on 3 October 2023, the European Union Agency for Asylum (EUAA) cited Amnesty International’s finding that in 2022, 0.4% of defendants in Russia were either acquitted or had their criminal proceedings ended on rehabilitating grounds. That figure combines those two outcomes and provides historical context for my account.

My account of missing digital assets and investigative concerns

Separately, a substantial amount of cryptocurrency was stolen from a BR Capital hot wallet used for routine operating expenses. Although significant in absolute terms, the affected balance was relatively limited compared with the fund’s wider assets; this was a theft, not a trading loss. Related incidents were reported to authorities in the United Kingdom and the Cayman Islands, and investigative work was carried out. My personal-wallet incidents are documented separately.

The fund’s broader holdings were protected by multi-signature arrangements for DeFi assets, distributed approvals for exchange accounts, and additional controls involving a fund administrator and custody arrangements. In my account, these safeguards prevented access to the wider pool of fund assets despite attempts to reach it.

I consider the underlying Russian prosecution one-sided in its handling of evidence supporting my defence. The wallet incidents also raise serious concerns for me about possible misconduct by Russian law-enforcement personnel. They lead me to question whether gaining access to my personal assets and, above all, to the fund’s assets was a motive for my detention in the first place. I do not present the perpetrators’ identities or criminal responsibility as established court findings. Redacted complaint records, investigative materials and supporting transaction evidence can be requested for restricted review, subject to approval and confidentiality.

Further legal context, my defence counsel’s assessment and supporting records can be requested through my office. Access to non-public materials is reviewed individually, subject to confidentiality and any necessary redactions.

A Personal Note

When I entered crypto, I understood the risks: money could be lost, projects could fail, and new ideas might never fulfil their promise. What I never imagined was losing my freedom or seeing my family pay such a painful price.

That experience changed how I see the world, but not what I believe in: the freedom to build, to control our own assets, to innovate and to transact across borders. Those convictions mean more to me now, not less. I have always been a builder, and I refuse to let what happened define the rest of my life. I have come through it stronger than ever — and I am still building.
